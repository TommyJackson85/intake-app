A File-Ready Checklist for Real-Estate Transaction Teams

Insights for Real-Estate Transaction Teams

A prospective client contacting the firm is not the same as a matter being ready to move forward.

In a busy real-estate practice, a new file can appear “open” long before the team has the information, documents, ownership, and visibility needed to progress it confidently. A client may have completed a form. An email may have arrived with attachments. A colleague may have entered an address into a system.

But if nobody can quickly answer what is missing, who owns the next action, and what could delay the transaction, the file is not truly ready.

A file-ready checklist gives the team a shared operational definition of readiness. It does not decide legal issues or replace firm procedures. It helps make the practical status of a matter visible before it is handed from intake into active transaction work.

Requirements vary by firm, matter type, transaction, client circumstances, jurisdiction, and professional judgment. The aim is not to create a universal legal checklist. It is to make repeated intake work more consistent, traceable, and easier to manage.

What “file-ready” means

There are several stages that can be confused:

Stage What it means What may still be missing
Initial contact A prospective client has contacted the firm Matter details, documents, conflict-review information, next owner
Basic intake Some contact and transaction information has been captured Documents, confirmations, parties, deadlines, internal review
In progress Requests and follow-ups are underway Clear accountability, complete records, unresolved exceptions
File-ready The team has completed its defined intake checks or can clearly see and manage approved exceptions Legal work still remains, but the intake handoff is controlled

“File-ready” does not mean “the transaction is risk-free” or “all professional work is complete.” It means the team has reached the point at which the matter can move into the next workflow stage with a clear record of what has been received, what remains outstanding, and who is responsible.

The operational file-ready checklist

Use the following as a starting point, then adapt it to the firm’s own procedures and the type of real-estate matter involved.

Expand each item below to match the firm’s procedures and the type of real-estate matter involved.

1. Client and contact information is captured

The team should be able to identify and contact the relevant client without searching through old messages.

Check that the file includes:

  • Primary client name
  • Preferred contact methods
  • Any alternate contacts or assistants where relevant

2. Matter type and high-level transaction context are recorded

The team should know what kind of matter this appears to be without reconstructing the story from email.

  • Purchase, sale, refinance, or another property-related engagement is noted at a high level
  • Enough context exists to choose the firm’s usual internal path

3. Key parties are identified where relevant

Known counterparties, entities, or related contacts should be listed early, and uncertainties should be flagged rather than assumed away.

  • Known parties are recorded in one place
  • Gaps in party information are marked for follow-up

4. Required initial documents are requested and tracked

Opening document requests should be intentional and visible, not buried only in a sent-mail folder.

  • The firm’s usual opening requests for this matter type have been sent
  • Each request has a status the team can see

5. Received documents are visible in one place

Materials already received should be findable without searching multiple threads, people, or attachment versions.

  • Received items can be located quickly
  • Obvious duplicates or unclear versions are noted

6. Missing information and documents are clearly identified

“Unknown” should be treated as a status, not as silence.

  • Outstanding items are written as a shared list
  • Missing items are visible before the handoff becomes urgent

7. The next owner or follow-up is assigned

Shared awareness without ownership still leaves gaps.

  • Someone is responsible for each open item
  • The next check-in is intentional rather than dependent on memory alone

8. Significant dates or time-sensitive items are recorded where applicable

Known dates the firm is tracking should be captured as operational facts, with unverified information marked for confirmation.

  • Relevant dates are recorded where known
  • Incomplete or unverified date information is flagged

9. Internal review is completed according to the firm’s workflow

The matter should pass through whatever intake or opening review the firm expects before the next stage, with the same completeness picture visible to the reviewer.

  • Required internal review has occurred
  • The reviewer can see what is complete and what remains open

10. Exceptions, uncertainties, and outstanding items are flagged before handoff

Known gaps should travel with the file instead of being rediscovered later.

  • Exceptions are recorded clearly
  • The receiving owner understands what is complete and what remains outstanding

What this checklist does not do

This checklist does not replace firm procedures, legal review, client-specific judgment, current jurisdiction-specific requirements, or professional responsibility for the matter. It does not determine rights, eligibility, compliance outcomes, or legal sufficiency. It does not guarantee that a matter is ready for closing or any other substantive milestone.

Its purpose is narrower: to help transaction teams organise operational readiness and make missing work visible before a handoff.

How to use this in practice

  1. Start with the team’s current process. Document what already happens on a typical residential or property-related matter.
  2. Identify repeated intake steps. Note the questions, documents, and checks that appear again and again.
  3. Turn those steps into a visible checklist. Focus on the items that regularly cause rework when they are missed.
  4. Assign a clear owner to each follow-up. Shared awareness without ownership still leaves gaps.
  5. Make missing items and blockers visible. A short outstanding list is more useful than a long email history.
  6. Improve the workflow using real examples of delays or rework. After a rushed matter, ask which checklist item would have surfaced the issue earlier—and update the list.

Over time, that habit turns intake from a pile of messages into a readiness conversation the whole team can join.

Key takeaway

A file is not ready simply because a client completed a form or replied to an email. It is closer to ready when the team can see what is missing, who owns the next step, and what still blocks the handoff.

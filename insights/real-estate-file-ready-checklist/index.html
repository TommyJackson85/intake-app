A File-Ready Checklist for Real-Estate Transaction Teams

A client contacting the firm is the beginning of a matter, not proof that the matter is ready for the next stage of work. In real-estate transaction teams, that distinction matters. Intake can feel complete because messages have been exchanged, while key facts, documents, ownership, or exceptions are still unclear.

This article offers an operational readiness checklist for lawyers, paralegals, legal assistants, and transaction coordinators. It is designed to help teams ask a practical question: can we see what is missing, who owns the next step, and what still blocks a clean handoff?

Requirements vary by firm, matter type, jurisdiction, transaction, and professional judgment. Treat the list below as a starting framework to adapt—not as a universal legal checklist or a substitute for your firm’s procedures.

First contact is not the same as file-ready

It helps to separate three stages that often get blurred in busy inboxes:

  • A prospective client has made contact — the firm knows someone needs help.
  • A client has provided some initial information — replies or forms have started to arrive.
  • A file is genuinely ready for the next stage — core details, document status, ownership, and exceptions are visible enough for a clean handoff.

First contact usually means the firm has enough information to begin a conversation. File-ready means the operational pieces needed for the next internal stage are visible and accounted for: core details captured, requested documents tracked, gaps identified, and follow-up ownership clear.

Teams that skip that distinction often discover problems late—during attorney review, closing preparation, condo/HOA document collection, or another time-sensitive handoff. A checklist will not remove every surprise. It can make the common ones visible earlier.

An operational file-ready checklist

Use these items as prompts for your own matter-opening workflow. Add, remove, or reorder them to match how your firm actually works.

1. Client and contact details captured

  • Primary client name and preferred contact methods are recorded in one place.
  • Any known alternate contacts or assistants are noted where relevant.

2. Matter type and high-level transaction context recorded

  • The team can tell what kind of matter this appears to be (for example, purchase, sale, refinance, or another property-related engagement).
  • Enough context exists to choose the right internal path without reconstructing the story from email.

3. Key parties identified where relevant

  • Known counterparties, entities, or related contacts are listed at a high level.
  • Uncertainties about parties are flagged instead of left as unspoken assumptions.

4. Required initial documents requested and tracked

  • The firm’s usual opening document requests for this matter type have been sent.
  • Each request has a visible status rather than living only in a sent-mail folder.

5. Received documents visible in one place

  • Materials already received can be found without searching multiple threads.
  • Obvious duplicates or unclear versions are noted for follow-up.

6. Missing information and documents clearly identified

  • Outstanding items are written down as a list the team can share.
  • “Unknown” is treated as a status, not as silence.

7. The next owner and follow-up are assigned

  • Someone is responsible for chasing each open item.
  • The next check-in is intentional rather than dependent on memory alone.

8. Significant dates or time-sensitive items recorded where applicable

  • Known dates the firm is tracking for this matter are captured as operational facts.
  • Unverified or incomplete date information is marked for confirmation by the firm.

9. Internal review completed according to the firm’s workflow

  • The matter has passed through whatever intake or opening review your firm expects before the next stage.
  • The reviewer can see the same completeness picture as the rest of the team.

10. Exceptions, uncertainties, and outstanding items flagged before handoff

  • Known gaps travel with the file instead of being rediscovered later.
  • The receiving owner understands what is complete and what remains open.

What this checklist does not do

This checklist does not replace firm procedures, legal review, client-specific judgment, current jurisdiction-specific requirements, or professional responsibility for the matter. It does not determine rights, eligibility, compliance outcomes, or legal sufficiency. It does not guarantee that a matter is ready for closing or any other substantive milestone.

Its purpose is narrower: to help transaction teams organise operational readiness and make missing work visible before a handoff.

How to use this in practice

  1. Start with the team’s current process. Document what already happens on a typical residential or property-related matter.
  2. Identify repeated intake steps. Note the questions, documents, and checks that appear again and again.
  3. Turn those steps into a visible checklist. Focus on the items that regularly cause rework when they are missed.
  4. Assign a clear owner to each follow-up. Shared awareness without ownership still leaves gaps.
  5. Make missing items and blockers visible. A short outstanding list is more useful than a long email history.
  6. Improve the workflow using real examples of delays or rework. After a rushed matter, ask which checklist item would have surfaced the issue earlier—and update the list.

Over time, that habit turns intake from a pile of messages into a readiness conversation the whole team can join.

Key takeaway

A file is not ready simply because a client completed a form or replied to an email. It is closer to ready when the team can see what is missing, who owns the next step, and what still blocks the handoff.
